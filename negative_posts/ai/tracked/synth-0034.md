After the conference: three things worth your time

The opening question session was more useful than I expected. Instead of letting the fastest hands dominate, the moderator collected questions throughout the morning and grouped them by topic. Beginners' questions received the same attention as proposals for language internals. I would recommend the recording even to people who usually skip keynotes.

The second keynote followed the history of a scientific computing project from a personal script to a shared tool. The speaker, Elena Ward, emphasized the unglamorous work: documenting decisions, responding to bug reports, and helping someone else make a first contribution. That history explained the project's success better than a tour of features would have.

Among the shorter sessions, I would start with the onboarding talk. The presenters showed a new engineer's first week as a sequence of small, observable tasks, including a test change and a production log investigation. I am going to borrow that structure for our next hire.

The data track offered a good progression from cleaning a public dataset to evaluating a classifier and then serving predictions asynchronously. It was refreshing to hear the operational constraints discussed alongside the model.

Some of the most useful conversations happened outside the rooms. At an informal analytics gathering, Samira Fox demonstrated a survival-analysis notebook and explained how censoring changes the interpretation of the curves. I left with a much clearer question for a project at work.

I stayed for one sprint day and repaired a documentation example that failed on a clean installation. The maintainer helped me reproduce the issue before touching the code. A modest patch, but a satisfying way to finish the event.
