How a small meetup became my first conference talk

I started Canal Street Analytics because I wanted a place to compare practical data problems with other people. The first gathering had eleven attendees, a borrowed screen, and no scheduled speaker. I filled the gap with a demonstration of a notebook that kept crashing during practice.

Nobody seemed to mind the rough edges. People asked useful questions, and two attendees offered to present at the following meetings. Within a few months, the group no longer depended on me supplying the content. That was the point at which it began to feel like a community.

Speaking there made a regional workshop less intimidating. I accepted a morning tutorial and an afternoon talk, which turned out to be too much preparation for one day. The sessions went acceptably, but I spent the breaks fixing examples instead of meeting people. I will choose one commitment next time.

For the national Python conference, I submitted a focused proposal about diagnosing a failing text pipeline. I also volunteered to review submissions. Reading other people's outlines was an education in making an audience and a learning outcome explicit. It showed me how many worthwhile proposals compete for limited space.

When my talk was selected, the organizers made the practical details clear: microphone check, room arrival time, and a volunteer who would signal the final minutes. Those arrangements removed a surprising amount of anxiety.

Afterward, a project maintainer stopped to ask about one example and suggested a simpler reproducer. That short conversation was the highlight of the trip. I left with a concrete improvement to make and enough confidence to submit another proposal next year.
