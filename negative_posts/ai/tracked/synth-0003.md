My short list for the Python conference has settled into six sessions. Most address something I have broken recently, which seems as good a selection method as any.

Sorting a crowded news feed — Mira Holt

Mira will build a small classifier for a discussion site, starting with readers' judgments of which submissions deserve attention. I want to see how she handles disagreement between readers. A filter that merely reproduces one person's irritation would be easy to build; a useful evaluation is the interesting part. The session uses ordinary Python text processing and scikit-learn rather than a large hosted service.

Public data, messy calendars — Owen Vale

This workshop follows bus arrival records from a municipal download through a pandas analysis. It includes duplicate readings, missing weekends, and timestamps that cross daylight saving changes. Those details make it especially appealing. I would rather watch someone repair a real dataset than admire another immaculate scatterplot.

What your encrypted connection actually promises — Celia Grant

The program describes a review of certificate validation, client settings, and server configuration. Celia will distinguish the protection offered by a working TLS connection from the broader claims people attach to it. I have a maintenance ticket involving an old client library, so I expect to leave with a more informed list of questions.

Repeatable deployments with Ansible — Tomas Reed

After a year of handwritten deployment notes, our team could use a demonstration of a repeatable process. This session covers inventory, configuration changes, and updating machines in batches. I am particularly interested in the failure halfway through an update, rather than the successful first run.

APIs after the first release — Anika Shore

Anika discusses versioning and client compatibility through examples from a reservation service. This sounds useful for anyone who has discovered that changing a response field can become a negotiation with five teams.

Experiments inside a game — Leon Finch

The final pick uses a simple 3D environment to study how people estimate motion. It promises enough implementation detail to explain how trial conditions are controlled. I do not work in psychology, but the combination of software design and experimental discipline is a good reason to make room for it.
