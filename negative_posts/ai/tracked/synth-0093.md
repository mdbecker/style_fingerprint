A keynote built around audience questions turned out to be the most useful general session I attended. Elise North grouped related submissions and invited people attending their first conference to ask the opening questions. The discussion still reached difficult language-design issues, but it began with concerns that a wider part of the room could recognize.

Another keynote traced a scientific analysis tool from a single laboratory script to a project maintained by several institutions. Peter Lark spent more time on installation, releases, and contributor support than on the original clever idea. That emphasis felt right. Software becomes infrastructure when people begin arranging their own work around it.

I have three recording recommendations for the technical program.

The onboarding workshop showed a newcomer completing a small production change in the first week. The presenters explained the support behind that outcome: a working development environment, a named guide, and a task whose dependencies were already resolved. I want our team to compare that process with the collection of links we currently send new hires.

The public-data tutorial analyzed restaurant inspection records. It included revised entries and establishments with several names, so the cleaning work was substantial. The presenter kept checks beside the transformations. It was easy to see how the final chart followed from the raw material.

The model evaluation session connected precision and recall with a human review queue. It also inspected mistakes rather than treating the score as the final answer. One input source accounted for a disproportionate share of missed cases; the overall result concealed that problem.

The short talks are worth a watch as a block. One demonstrated a race condition with a tiny program, and another repaired an error message that had misled users for years. Their limited time encouraged unusually concrete explanations.

At lunch I joined a discussion on survival analysis using equipment repair histories. The demonstration distinguished a machine observed to fail from one still operating when observation ended. That gave me a useful way into a subject I had previously avoided.

I stayed for a contributor day and fixed a broken documentation example. A maintainer helped me reproduce the failure and choose an appropriate regression test. The change was small, but the experience removed much of the uncertainty around contributing to a project I use daily.
