The conference made me rethink how we support people entering Python, whether they arrive through a school, a new job, or an open source project.

A teacher in the education session described maintaining a computing course while also covering two other subjects. Her request was modest: time to prepare lessons and someone dependable to call when the lab failed. It made several corporate outreach programs look rather thin. A donated laptop helps only if the school can keep the class running.

The keynote questions session took a similarly deliberate approach to participation. Ravi North asked attendees to submit questions before the talk and used the first ten minutes to answer those from newcomers. There were still deep language design questions, but fewer people had to fight for microphone time. I found the format more useful than the usual sequence of speeches disguised as questions.

Later, Clara West explained how a scientific notebook tool moved from personal convenience to shared infrastructure. The milestones were mundane and important: an installation guide, reliable issue triage, and somebody other than the original author able to publish a release. I would recommend the recording to anyone whose side project now has users.

My technical picks are the onboarding workshop and the two data sessions on evaluation. In the onboarding workshop, a new engineer's first change was intentionally small but went through the full release process. That seems better preparation than a large isolated practice exercise. One evaluation talk examined a classifier that performed well on long documents and poorly on short ones. The other connected prediction thresholds with the amount of work a review team could absorb.

The short talks deserved their own afternoon. A five-minute demonstration of a broken timezone assumption taught me more than some hour-long introductions.

Outside the program, I joined a small discussion about survival analysis. A developer showed how censored observations change the meaning of a familiar-looking curve, using equipment maintenance as the example. That is now on my reading list.

I finished with one day of project work. A maintainer helped me improve an error message and add a regression test. It was a small contribution, but I left knowing how to build the project, run its checks, and ask a useful question in an issue. That may be the most durable thing I brought home.
