Three sessions from the Python conference stayed with me after the trip home. Each made a familiar problem look rather less inevitable.

Access starts before the interview

The opening education talk, given by Mara Quinn, focused on the students who never get far enough to apply for a software job. She described schools where computing is offered only as an extracurricular activity, with transport and equipment costs deciding who can attend. Her proposed changes were practical: paid preparation time for teachers, lessons that run on school equipment, and clubs scheduled before the last bus leaves.

That is a more demanding commitment than sending volunteers for a single afternoon. It also seems much likelier to make a lasting difference. I would like employers funding local coding events to consider paying for teacher support as well.

A questions session with room to think

I expected a conventional keynote from Daniel Moss. Instead, he put audience questions on a screen, grouped duplicates, and asked people who had not yet spoken to take the microphone first. The discussion covered release decisions, compatibility, and the difficulty of saying no to a plausible feature request.

The format worked because he allowed some answers to be uncertain. Watching a maintainer weigh an awkward tradeoff was more useful than hearing a polished account of how everything had gone according to plan.

A tool becomes a community

Elena Rook's scientific computing keynote began with a script she wrote to check an experiment. The interesting transition came when colleagues began relying on it. Tests, documentation, and a release process became necessary because other people's work now depended on her choices. That story connected neatly with a later session on onboarding new contributors.

For recordings, I would start with the onboarding workshop, the public transport data analysis, and the tutorial on evaluating a text classifier. The onboarding presenters supplied an actual first-week checklist, including a small task a newcomer could complete without waiting for production access. The analysis session showed failed hypotheses as well as useful plots. The classifier tutorial spent enough time on the test set to explain why a striking score might be misleading.

I still have a long watch list. These recommendations are the sessions I attended, rather than a judgment on the full program.
